Joseon Korea, 1434 · an automatic water clock

자격루 (Jagyeokru)The water clock that strikes itself

From the first day of the seventh lunar month of 1434, King Sejong's court kept time with a new water clock.1 It announced the time without human help. Rising water lifted a rod, the rod let small copper balls drop, and the balls set off levers that made wooden figures strike a bell, a drum and a gong.1 The model below follows the description in the Veritable Records of King Sejong, step by step.

01How the clock works

The Veritable Records entry for the first day of the seventh month of Sejong's 16th year describes the clock in detail.1 Sejong ordered a new clock because the old one was imprecise, and he worried that the people calling out the time would make mistakes. So he had Jang Yeong-sil, then holding the military post of hogun, build wooden figures that would announce the time by themselves, without human help.1 Other accounts also name Yi Cheon and Kim Jo among the makers.23

Water: four supply vessels, two receivers

There were four supply vessels of different sizes and two receiving vessels, used in turn when the water was changed. The receivers were 11 cheok 2 chon long and 1 cheok 8 chon across, in traditional Korean units.1 Several supply vessels kept the water pressure steady and the two receivers avoided gaps, which kept the error small. The clock was also checked against instruments such as the sun-and-star time-determining instrument.3

Both receivers had their balls set in advance. When one was full, the pipe was moved to the other straight away, and the full one was drained and set again while the second one kept time.3

The float rod and the copper plates

A float rod 10 cheok 2 chon long rose in the receiver. Its face was divided into the 12 double-hours of the day, each split into 8 marks; with the extra parts of each hour's first (初) and second (正) halves, a day came to 100 marks.1 A hollow square wooden column stood on the vessel. On its left was a copper plate with 12 holes holding small copper balls the size of bullets, one for each double-hour. On the right, a second plate had 25 holes for the night's watches and points. There were 12 plates in all, one for each of the 12 rods, swapped through the year by solar term.1

Small balls, iron balls and spoons

As the rod rose, it tripped the mechanism at the next hole, and a small ball fell into a copper tube. Every hole in the tube started open. The first ball dropped through the first hole, and the mechanism it struck closed that hole behind it. The next ball rolled over the closed hole to the next one, and so on down the tube.1

Each mechanism held back an iron ball the size of an egg. They sat on four partitions laid out like a corridor: 12 on the left for the hours, 5 in the middle for the watches and each watch's first point, and 20 on the right for the remaining points.1

The mechanism opens and the big ball drops, rolls into the short tube hung under the seat and, as it falls, moves the spoon-like device. Its other end pushes up from inside the tube against the arm of the figure in charge of the hour, and the bell rings.

A watch ball first pushed the watch figure's arm to sound the drum. It then rolled on into the point tube and turned the first-point mechanism, so the point figure sounded the gong.1

The figures and the wheel of twelve

The east bay of a three-bay pavilion held a seat on two levels.1 On the upper level stood three figures. One kept the hours and struck a bell, one kept the watches and struck a drum, and one kept the points and struck a gong. Under the middle level was a horizontal wheel with twelve figures around it. Each stood on an iron stem so it could rise and fall, and each held the plaque for its hour.1

The hour ball then rolled on and fell onto the north end of a beam 6 cheok 6 chon long. Its south end rose and, through a wooden post, lifted that hour's figure above the middle level. When the hour ended, the figure returned to the wheel and the next hour's figure came up in its place.1 The Records add that all the machinery was hidden, so visitors saw only the wooden figures in their caps and belts.1

02Hours, watches and points

The day had 12 double-hours (時), each named for one of the twelve earthly branches: 子 for the Rat, 丑 for the Ox, 寅 for the Tiger and so on. The twelve figures on the wheel stood for them.14 Each double-hour lasted two modern hours, so 子時 ran from 23:00 to 01:00. Each was split into a first half, 初, and a second half, 正.1 Night was counted separately, in five watches (更), each split into five points (點). That is why the right-hand plate had 25 holes.1

Watches were a share of the night, not a fixed length, so a winter watch ran longer than a summer one.3 Instead of 21 separate night rods, the new clock used 12, each covering two solar terms.12

03Carrying the time across Seoul

To be heard beyond the pavilion, the signal was relayed by drummers at the palace gates. Bell-drums stood at the south gate of Gyeonghoeru, at Wolhwamun and at Geunjeongmun, and a great bell-drum at Gwanghwamun. At night, whoever kept each gate's drum heard the wooden figure and passed the signal on in turn.1

Students of the Office of Astronomy kept the drums at the Gyeonghoeru south gate, Yeongchumun and Gwanghwamun. Soldiers on night duty at the other gates kept theirs. At noon (午時) a large drum at Yeongchumun answered the figure's drum and Gwanghwamun passed it on.1

Namuwiki adds the last step. At the third watch (三更), roughly 23:00 to 01:00, the clock's drum sounded three times. The gatekeepers at the palace's main gate beat the gate-tower drum three times, and the drummer at Jonggak, the city bell pavilion, beat three more, so the sound spread across the city.3

04Six centuries of the clock

  1. 1398King Taejo, year 7

    A water clock called Gyeongnu is made, and a bell is struck to mark the night hours.3

  2. 1433King Sejong, year 15

    Sejong credits Jang Yeong-sil with a self-striking palace clock and promotes him to hogun. Jang had been a government slave of Dongnae County.12

  3. 1434Sejong 16, 7th month, day 1

    The new clock goes into use in the Borugak pavilion at Gyeongbokgung Palace, south of Gyeonghoeru, and becomes the dynasty's standard clock.123

  4. 1438King Sejong, year 20

    A second automatic clock, the Ongnu, is installed in the Heumgyeonggak pavilion.2

  5. 1455King Danjong, year 3

    The automatic striking is stopped, 21 years after it began. Namuwiki suggests that with its makers gone, nobody could repair it.3

  6. 1469King Yejong, year 1

    The clock runs again.3

  7. 1505Yeonsangun, year 11

    The clock moves to Changdeokgung Palace, into a newly built Borugak.3

  8. 1536King Jungjong, year 31

    Its strikes no longer match the time, so a new clock is begun in 1534. The craftsman Bak Se-ryong finishes it in 1536.34

  9. 1550–1652

    Repaired in 1550, 1601 and 1652, 51 years apart each time.3

  10. 1938

    Under Japanese rule, the surviving vessels are moved to the Gwangmyeongmun gate at Deoksugung Palace for display.4

  11. 1985August 9

    Designated National Treasure No. 229.34

  12. 2018–2020

    Conservation work corrects earlier readings of the names cast into the vessels and finds that the dragon reliefs were cast by the lost-wax method. The vessels then go on display at the National Palace Museum of Korea.3

  13. 2021June 29

    Parts of the ball-release system, known until then only from the Records, are excavated together with Hangul metal type and other instruments.3

  14. 2024

    A ball-release system rebuilt from those finds is added to the full-size replica at the National Science Museum.3

05What survives

The vessels of the 1536 clock survive as National Treasure No. 229, the Clepsydra of Changgyeonggung Palace, also known as Borugak Jagyeokru (officially romanized Jagyeongnu).34 The ball machinery is gone and only the water vessels remain.34

The largest vessel is inscribed with its casting date, the sixth month of 1536. The two cylindrical vessels carry the titles and names of the officials and craftsmen who made the clock.3 Two stone stands that held the receivers are said to remain behind Myeongjeongjeon hall at Changgyeonggung Palace.4

The Korea Heritage Service's description notes that a Ming dynasty water clock surviving in Guangdong is slightly older, but rates this one as especially valuable for its size and fine workmanship.3 The clock also appeared on the front of the old 10,000 won banknote.4
